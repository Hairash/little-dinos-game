# Legal & Compliance

Status of the project against privacy, IP, security and commercial regulation,
with a prioritised action list.

**This is not legal advice.** It is an engineering-side risk assessment written
from the code and the live deployment. At the current scale (free, hobby, no
revenue) templates and self-service are proportionate. Get a lawyer before the
first paid transaction.

- **Last reviewed:** 2026-09-12
- **Reviewed against:** commit `a07ae0a`, branch `release/2`
- **Live deployment:** frontend `https://little-dinos-game.netlify.app/`,
  backend `https://little-dinos-backend.fly.dev/` (Fly.io, `cdg`/Paris)

## 2. Cookies and the ePrivacy Directive

**No cookie banner is required, and adding a "consent or leave" wall would be
worse than doing nothing.**

Reasoning:

1. ePrivacy Art. 5(3) (Spain: LSSI-CE Art. 22.2) requires consent for storing or
   accessing data on a user's device **unless it is strictly necessary for a
   service explicitly requested by the user.**
2. Every `localStorage` key in this project is either authentication or
   functional game state. Nothing is analytics, profiling, or advertising. The
   exemption applies to all of it.
3. A "we use cookies — if you disagree, please leave" banner is a **cookie
   wall**. Consent obtained that way is not freely given (GDPR Art. 4(11) and
   Art. 7(4)), and cookie walls are a standard enforcement target for the AEPD.
   The idea was to avoid building accept/reject logic — the correct way to avoid
   that logic is to not need consent at all, which is where the project already
   is.

**What changes this:** adding Google Analytics, a Meta Pixel, an embedded
YouTube player (`youtube.com` sets cookies; `youtube-nocookie.com` still sets
storage on play), or any ad network. Then a real banner with a working *Reject*
that defaults to off, blocking the script until consent, becomes mandatory.

**Recommendation:** if analytics are wanted, use a cookieless, non-tracking
provider (Plausible, Umami, Fathom) with an IP-anonymising configuration. Still
disclose it in the privacy policy; still no banner.

One line belongs in the privacy policy regardless: *"This site uses no cookies.
It stores your login token and game progress in your browser's local storage,
which is required for the game to work."*

## 3. GDPR obligations

### 3.1 Privacy policy — **missing, mandatory, highest priority**

GDPR Arts. 13–14 require the information to be given **at the point of
collection**, i.e. on the signup screen. There is currently no privacy policy on
the site at all. This is the single hard blocker before advertising.

Must state:

- Controller identity and a contact address (see §6 on whose address that is)
- Categories of data (§1 above)
- Purposes and legal bases:
  - Account, authentication, gameplay, saved maps → **Art. 6(1)(b) contract**
  - Server logs, abuse prevention, security → **Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests**
  - *No consent-based processing exists today, which is why there is no banner*
- Recipients: the four processors in §1, plus *"your username is visible to other
  players in any game you join"*
- Retention periods — currently undefined; see §3.4
- Data subject rights (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability,
  objection) and how to exercise them
- The right to lodge a complaint with the **AEPD** (`www.aepd.es`)
- That no international transfer happens outside the processors' SCC/DPF coverage
- That the service is not intended for children under 14 (§4)

Link it from the signup form, the main menu, and a site footer.

### 3.2 Terms of Service — missing, strongly recommended

Not strictly mandated for a free service, but without one there is no warranty
disclaimer, no liability cap, no stated right to terminate an abusive account,
no username conduct rules, and no governing law. Template-cheap; write it
alongside the privacy policy.

Must include a minimum-age statement and username rules (no impersonation, no
offensive handles, no personal data in usernames).

### 3.3 Data subject rights — no mechanism exists

There is no account-deletion path and no data export. At this scale a
documented manual process (*"email us and we will delete your account within 30
days"*) satisfies Arts. 15 and 17, **provided it is written in the policy and
actually honoured**. A self-serve *Delete account* button is better, is not much
work, and removes the ongoing manual burden.

Note the interaction with `Move` and `Game`: deleting a user cascades
(`on_delete=CASCADE` on `GamePlayer`, `Move`, `SavedMap`) but `Game.turn_player`
is `SET_NULL`, so games survive with a null turn player. Verify that deleting a
mid-game participant does not corrupt live games before shipping a delete button.

### 3.4 Retention — undefined

Nothing is ever deleted. `Move` rows accumulate forever, finished `Game` rows
are never pruned. GDPR requires defining a period (Art. 5(1)(e)).

Proposal: purge `Game` and `Move` rows 12 months after the game ends; delete
accounts inactive for 24 months after a warning — which is impossible without an
email address, so in practice: state a retention period for game data, and treat
accounts as retained until the user requests deletion. State exactly that.

### 3.5 Records of processing (Art. 30)

Fewer than 250 people and no high-risk processing means the full Art. 30
obligation is largely relieved, but the exemption is narrow. Keep a one-page
internal record covering the table in §1. Cheap insurance in an AEPD inquiry.

### 3.6 Breach notification

Art. 33: notify the AEPD within **72 hours** of becoming aware of a personal-data
breach. Art. 34: notify affected users directly if the risk to them is high —
**which is impossible without email addresses.** The fallback is a public notice
on the site, which Art. 34(3)(c) permits when direct contact requires
disproportionate effort. Note this in the privacy policy so the fallback is
pre-established.

### 3.7 Not required

- **DPO** — no large-scale monitoring, no special-category data.
- **Art. 27 EU representative** — you are established in the EU.
- **DPIA** — no systematic monitoring or high-risk profiling. Revisit if
  behavioural analytics or ad targeting are ever added.

### 3.8 On relocating within the EU

The lead supervisory authority follows your main establishment. Moving from
Spain to another member state changes the authority named in the privacy policy
and the applicable child-consent age (§4). Update the policy when you move, and
note that Spanish residency permits are national — see §6.3.

## 4. Children

**The highest-consequence risk in the project.** Cartoon dinosaurs, simple turn-based
rules and a free web build describe a product that children will play, whether or
not they are the target.

### GDPR Art. 8 — Spain sets the age at 14

Spain's LOPDGDD Art. 7 sets the digital consent age at **14** (the GDPR range is
13–16 and member states differ — Germany and the Netherlands use 16). Below that
age, processing based on consent needs parental authorisation.

Mitigating factor: this project's legal basis for account data is **contract**,
not consent, which reduces but does not remove the Art. 8 exposure. Contracts
with minors have their own validity problems under Spanish civil law.

### COPPA (US) — the expensive one

If the service is deemed *"directed to children"* under 13, COPPA requires
**verifiable parental consent** before collecting anything — a process that is
genuinely costly and impractical for a hobby project. The FTC weighs subject
matter, visual style, animated characters, and the actual audience. A cartoon
dinosaur game is squarely in the risk zone. The 2025 COPPA rule amendments
tightened retention limits and third-party disclosure further.

### Mitigation (do all of these)

1. **ToS states a minimum age of 14** (16 if you relocate to a member state that
   sets 16), with a plain statement that the service is not directed to children
   under 13 and that accounts believed to belong to them will be deleted.
2. **A neutral age gate at signup** — a date-of-birth field, not a "Are you 13?
   Yes/No" checkbox, which regulators treat as no gate at all. Do not store the
   date; store only the boolean result.
3. **Keep data minimisation** — the absence of email collection is the single
   strongest COPPA-risk reducer the project has. Do not add email collection
   casually.
4. **Never add ad SDKs, behavioural analytics, or persistent tracking
   identifiers.** Under COPPA these are what turn "we collect almost nothing"
   into a violation.
5. **Do not market to children.** The advertising channel and creative matter as
   much as the product: Instagram/YouTube campaigns that visibly target kids are
   evidence that the service is child-directed.

### UK

If UK traffic becomes meaningful, the ICO's Age Appropriate Design Code applies
to services likely to be accessed by children. Its default requirements (privacy
by default, no nudge techniques, data minimisation) are already largely met here.

## 5. Security findings

Assessed against GDPR Art. 32 ("appropriate technical and organisational
measures"), not just as engineering hygiene. Existing documentation lives in
`docs/security-guidelines.md`; these are the gaps that remain.

### 5.1 Insecure production defaults fail silently — **critical**

`backend/server/settings.py:41` ships a hardcoded `django-insecure-…` fallback
for `DJANGO_SECRET_KEY`, `JWT_SECRET_KEY` falls back to it (`:46`), and
`ALLOWED_HOSTS` defaults to `"*"` (`:51`).

If a Fly secret is ever unset, rotated wrongly, or lost during an app rebuild,
production serves with a **publicly known JWT signing key** — every token
forgeable by anyone reading this repository, for any account. The failure is
silent; nothing in the boot sequence objects.

**Fix:** when `DEBUG` is false, raise `ImproperlyConfigured` on a missing
`DJANGO_SECRET_KEY`, a missing `JWT_SECRET_KEY`, or an `ALLOWED_HOSTS` of `*`.
Fail to boot rather than fail open.

### 5.2 JWT: 365-day lifetime, no revocation

`backend/server/utils/jwt.py:16` sets a one-year expiry. `signout` in
`backend/server/auth.py` returns `{"ok": true}` and does nothing server-side —
the token remains valid for the rest of the year after "logging out". Tokens
live in `localStorage`, so any XSS yields a year-long account takeover, and
there is no way to invalidate a leaked token short of rotating `JWT_SECRET_KEY`
and logging out every user simultaneously.

**Minimum fix:** add a `token_version` integer to the user, embed it in the
payload, and verify it on decode. Incrementing it revokes that user's tokens.
**Better:** shorten to ~30 days with a refresh flow.

This is already documented as a known trade-off in `docs/security-guidelines.md`;
the trade-off is defensible for expiry length but not for the total absence of a
revocation mechanism.

### 5.3 No rate limiting on authentication

`/auth/signin/` and `/auth/signup/` in `backend/server/auth.py` accept unlimited
attempts. Two consequences: unthrottled credential stuffing against PBKDF2
hashes, and **username enumeration** — signup returns `"username taken"` and
signin returns 401, so the two responses distinguish existing accounts.

**Fix:** throttle per IP and per username (django-ratelimit, or Fly-level).
Enumeration is hard to close fully on a username-based system; throttling is the
proportionate mitigation.

### 5.4 Django admin publicly reachable

`https://little-dinos-backend.fly.dev/admin/` responds (302 to the login form,
verified 2026-09-12). It is session-authenticated, has no 2FA and no IP
restriction. A superuser compromise exposes every account and every game record —
a reportable personal-data breach under Art. 33.

**Fix:** restrict by IP, move to an unguessable path, or disable the admin URL in
production and use `fly ssh console` for administration.

### 5.5 Missing security headers on the frontend

Live headers on `little-dinos-game.netlify.app` carry HSTS (Netlify default) but
**no `Content-Security-Policy`, no `X-Frame-Options`, no `Referrer-Policy`, no
`X-Content-Type-Options`**. `frontend/netlify.toml` sets cache headers only.

CSP matters more than usual here: it is the main defence-in-depth against the
`localStorage` token-theft scenario in §5.2.

**Fix:** add to `netlify.toml` —

```toml
[[headers]]
  for = "/*"
  [headers.values]
    Content-Security-Policy = "default-src 'self'; img-src 'self' data:; font-src 'self'; connect-src 'self' https://little-dinos-backend.fly.dev wss://little-dinos-backend.fly.dev; frame-ancestors 'none'; base-uri 'self'; object-src 'none'"
    X-Frame-Options = "DENY"
    X-Content-Type-Options = "nosniff"
    Referrer-Policy = "strict-origin-when-cross-origin"
    Permissions-Policy = "geolocation=(), microphone=(), camera=()"
```

Test the CSP carefully against Vite's output before relying on it — the build may
need `'unsafe-inline'` for styles, which should be narrowed with a nonce or hash
rather than left broad.

The backend is missing HSTS (`SECURE_HSTS_SECONDS` unset); `fly.toml` has
`force_https` so the redirect is handled, but the header is still worth adding.

### 5.6 No password reset, no user contact channel

A consequence of collecting no email. Users who forget their password are
permanently locked out with no recovery path, and there is no channel for the
Art. 34 breach notification in §3.6.

**Options:** (a) accept it, document it at signup — *"there is no password
recovery, choose carefully"*; (b) add an **optional** email field used only for
reset and security notices, disclosed in the privacy policy. Option (b) adds
personal data but is the honest answer for a service meant to retain users.

### 5.7 Verified as sound

- Django ORM used exclusively, no raw SQL — no injection surface
- No `v-html` with user-controlled content
- PBKDF2-SHA256 password hashing with the four default Django validators
- Server-side fog-of-war filtering prevents state disclosure to opponents
- Game access control checks player membership
- CSRF exemptions are correctly reasoned — JWT in an `Authorization` header is
  not automatically attached by browsers
- Backend security headers from Django's `SecurityMiddleware` are present in
  production (verified live)
- `MAX_START_PAYLOAD_BYTES` and the map dimension bounds in
  `backend/game/views.py` cap hostile client-supplied JSON

## 6. Intellectual property

### 6.1 AI-generated art — the 171 images

All art is AI-generated. Three separate consequences, only one of which is a
launch blocker.

**(a) The assets are probably not protected by copyright.** The US Copyright
Office holds that material generated purely by AI from a prompt lacks the human
authorship copyright requires (*Zarya of the Dawn*, 2023; the Office's 2025
copyrightability report; *Thaler v. Perlmutter*). EU law requires the work to be
"the author's own intellectual creation", which prompt-only output is unlikely to
satisfy.

Practical effect: **you can use them, but you probably cannot stop anyone else
from copying them.** A competitor could lift every sprite. This is not a legal
risk to you — it is a limit on what you own. It also substantially reduces the
stakes of the MIT-licence question in §6.3, since the art may be effectively
unprotectable regardless of what the licence file says.

If asset exclusivity ever matters commercially, the fix is human modification:
meaningful hand-editing creates protectable authorship in the edited layer.

**(b) The generator's terms of service govern commercial use — verify this.**
This varies sharply and is the one item that could bite:

| Generator | Commercial position |
|---|---|
| Midjourney (paid) | You own the assets; Midjourney retains a broad licence. Pro tier required above $1M company revenue. |
| **Midjourney (free tier)** | Assets are **CC BY-NC 4.0 — non-commercial only.** Would block monetisation and arguably conflicts with MIT redistribution. |
| OpenAI (DALL·E / GPT image) | Terms assign output rights to the user; commercial use permitted. |
| Adobe Firefly | Commercially safe, trained on licensed content, enterprise indemnification. |
| Stable Diffusion (local) | CreativeML OpenRAIL-M; outputs are yours, with use restrictions. |

**Action:** identify which service produced the images and confirm the tier.
Record it in this document. If any came from a free Midjourney tier, they must be
regenerated before monetisation.

**(c) Output similarity to protected characters.** AI models reproduce training
data closely when prompted toward well-known properties. Copyright and trade
dress in a *specific character design* are enforceable regardless of how the
image was made. Review `dino1`–`dino8` and the building assets against Yoshi
(Nintendo), Dino (Flintstones / WBD), *The Land Before Time* characters
(Universal), Barney, and Dino Crisis. Similarity of *idea* (a cartoon dinosaur)
is fine; similarity of *expression* (silhouette, colour scheme, distinctive
features) is not.

**(d) Disclosure.** No obligation for a web build. Steam requires an AI-content
disclosure at submission, and console/mobile storefronts increasingly do. Note it
if the game ever ships to a storefront.

### 6.2 Trademark — direct answers

> **Should I make sure the name isn't taken?**

**Yes, and do it before spending anything on advertising.** Searching is free;
rebranding after building an audience costs the audience. Check:

- **EUIPO eSearch plus** — Nice Class 9 (game software) and Class 41
  (entertainment services)
- **USPTO TESS** — same classes, if US traffic matters
- **OEPM** (Spain) for national marks
- Steam, itch.io, App Store, Google Play, and plain web search for unregistered
  marks — **unregistered use can still block you** through passing off and
  earlier-rights claims in many jurisdictions
- Domain and social handle availability

> **Should I register it?**

**Not required to publish. Worth doing at one of two triggers:** the first paid
transaction, or the point where the audience is large enough that losing the name
would hurt. Costs: EUIPO ~€850 for one class (+€50 second, +€150 third, 10-year
term); USPTO ~$250–350 per class; OEPM Spain ~€150 for national-only.

> **On "Little Dinos" specifically**

It is **descriptive and therefore weak**. That cuts both ways: hard for you to
register or defend, and easy for a holder of a stronger dinosaur-related mark in
Class 9/41 to object. Given the stated flexibility on the name, this is an
argument for choosing an **invented or arbitrary word** instead — distinctive
names clear searches more easily, register more cheaply, and are actually
defensible. Renaming today costs a string constant in `frontend/index.html` and a
Netlify subdomain. Renaming in a year costs the audience.

Also: move off `little-dinos-game.netlify.app` to a domain you control before
advertising. A free platform subdomain is not a brand, it cannot be moved between
hosts, and you cannot register a trademark around it.

### 6.3 Licensing — MIT stays, with a clarification

Decision: **keep the project open source.**

Two things to fix in `LICENSE`:

1. **Scope MIT to the code and state the asset position separately.** Right now
   MIT arguably covers the art too, which is at best ambiguous. Given §6.1(a),
   the practical honest statement is that the images are AI-generated and may not
   attract copyright. Say so rather than asserting rights you may not have.
2. **`Copyright (c) 2023 Hairash` is a pseudonym.** Use a legal name or, later, a
   company name. A pseudonymous copyright holder is awkward to enforce and
   awkward to identify as the controller in a privacy policy.

Also understand the consequence you are accepting: **MIT permits anyone to fork,
rebrand and sell this game, including the paid-cosmetics business.** That is
compatible with open source, not with exclusivity. If exclusivity ever matters,
the usual pattern is open-source client + proprietary server-side entitlements,
where the paid content lives behind an account rather than in the repository.

Note that MIT cannot be revoked for commits already published — anyone may fork
from the existing history. Any licence change applies going forward only.

### 6.4 Font

`frontend/public/RocknRollOne-Regular.ttf` is a Google Font under SIL OFL 1.1.
Free for commercial use, embeddable. Keep the OFL licence text alongside it; do
not sell the font itself. No action beyond including the licence file.

## 7. Business structure, tax and immigration status

Nothing here blocks publishing a free game. All of it blocks **monetisation**.

### 7.1 Residency permit — check this first

**Your Spanish residency permit type determines whether you may earn money from
this at all.** A permit tied to employment (*cuenta ajena*) does **not** authorise
self-employment. Earning revenue from the game under such a permit is a
work-authorisation violation with immigration consequences far more serious than
any tax or GDPR issue in this document.

If monetisation is the plan, verify whether your permit covers *cuenta propia*,
and if not, apply to modify it or obtain dual authorisation before the first
euro. **Russian citizenship is not itself a barrier** — legal EU residents may
conduct business; residency status, not nationality, is the gating factor.

Relatedly: Spanish residency permits are national. Relocating to another EU
member state means re-establishing residency there, unless you hold EU long-term
resident status.

### 7.2 Employment contract with CloudBlue

Free to check, potentially the most expensive item on this list if ignored.
Spanish employment contracts commonly include IP-assignment clauses covering
work created during employment and non-compete or side-activity disclosure
obligations. Read the contract's IP and *pacto de no competencia* clauses. If the
game is built partly on work time or work equipment, the exposure is higher.

### 7.3 While the game stays free

No company, no tax registration, no VAT. A non-commercial personal project run by
an individual is fine as-is in Spain. The obligations that *do* apply now are the
privacy ones in §3.

One Spanish specific: **LSSI-CE (Ley 34/2002) requires an *aviso legal***
(identity, contact, and registration details) for information-society services.
Its scope centres on economic activity, so a genuinely free hobby game is a weak
case for enforcement — but the AEPD reads "economic activity" broadly, and the
privacy policy already needs a contact address. Publishing a combined *aviso
legal* + privacy policy page costs nothing extra and closes the question.

### 7.4 At monetisation

| Item | Action |
|---|---|
| Business registration | Register as **autónomo** (Hacienda modelo 036/037 + RETA) **before the first invoice**, or form an **SL**. Autónomo: low setup, *tarifa plana* reduced quota in year one, unlimited personal liability. SL: ~€3,000 setup plus ongoing accounting, liability capped, and a company address to publish instead of your home address. |
| VAT | Digital services to EU consumers are taxed in the customer's country. Register for **OSS** (modelo 369) to file one return instead of 27. |
| Payments | Use a **Merchant of Record** — Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, FastSpring — which becomes the seller of record and absorbs EU VAT and US sales tax entirely. Strongly preferred over raw Stripe at this scale. Never store card data. |
| Payment-provider KYC | Expect identity verification and sanctions screening. Russian citizenship draws additional scrutiny at onboarding even for EU residents. Use a Spanish/EU bank account; avoid routing anything through Russian financial institutions. |
| Consumer withdrawal right | EU grants 14 days on digital content **unless** the buyer expressly consents to immediate performance and acknowledges losing the right. This must be a checkbox in the purchase flow, not a ToS clause. |
| Loot boxes | **Do not build them.** Restricted or banned in Belgium and the Netherlands, under active EU scrutiny elsewhere, and a direct trigger for child-protection enforcement. Direct-purchase cosmetics avoid the entire problem. |
| In-game currency | Avoid. 2025 EU CPC guidance targets exactly this pattern — obscured real-money pricing, dark patterns, and pressure on minors. Price cosmetics in real currency. |
| Age ratings | IARC (→ PEGI/ESRB) if publishing to app stores or Steam. Not required for a pure web build. |
| EU Accessibility Act | In force since 28 June 2025 for consumer e-commerce. Microenterprises (<10 staff, <€2M turnover) are exempt — revisit only at real scale. |

### 7.5 Digital Services Act

Minimal exposure. There is **no chat and no user-to-user content sharing** —
`list_saved_maps` is scoped to the owning user, so the only content visible
between players is usernames. Combined with micro-enterprise exemptions, the
practical obligations reduce to having a contact point and terms, both already
required above.

This changes if chat, public map sharing, or a leaderboard with free-text fields
is ever added — those create user-generated content and with it notice-and-action
obligations.

## 8. Advertising and marketing

Nothing legally blocks an Instagram or YouTube campaign once §9's P0 items are
done. Constraints to respect:

- **Music and footage in promo videos must be licensed.** Content ID will catch
  unlicensed music, and a takedown against a paid campaign wastes the spend.
- **No third-party trademarks or characters** in creative.
- **Influencer partnerships require disclosure** (#ad / paid partnership) under
  both EU consumer law and platform rules.
- **Do not target children** — see §4. The targeting parameters chosen in the ad
  platform are evidence of who the service is directed to.
- **Email marketing requires opt-in.** Not applicable today (no addresses
  collected), and a reason to think carefully before adding §5.6 option (b).
